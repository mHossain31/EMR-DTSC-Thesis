## Title

Emission-aware rerouting in a simulated New York City district: an audit, a preregistered test, and the role of network coding

## Authors

Mohammed Y. Hossain, Billy M. Kyaw

## Abstract (1787 of 1920 characters)

Microscopic simulation is widely used to estimate how much rerouting could lower urban traffic emissions, but such estimates are only as good as the protocol and the network behind them. A 2024 student project reported that machine-learning-triggered rerouting cut simulated NOx in Flushing, Queens, by 71.53%. Auditing its notebook, we find that the comparison set a run of about 4,132 s against runs stopped after 1000 s and that its reroute calls returned unchanged routes; replicated under a matched protocol on 60 seeds per demand level, the same rule is equivalent to no rerouting. We then test emission-aware rerouting driven by a 60-second traffic-state forecast in SUMO under a preregistered, paired, multi-seed design with travel-time and placebo baselines. On the network as built from OpenStreetMap it changed total NOx by -9.75%, -11.00% and -14.95% against no rerouting and by -9.25%, -8.49% and -8.07% against travel-time rerouting (light, moderate, heavy demand), at a travel-time cost of a few percent. The reduction comes mostly from moving traffic onto residential and service streets, whose NOx changed by +90%, +95% and +94%, and the forecaster is worse than a per-edge historical average of the emission rate it supplies. In a sensitivity experiment specified before its runs, recoding the network with New York City's default 25 mph limit and stop control at minor-street junctions reversed the effect of measured-emission rerouting against travel-time rerouting (+2.9%, +3.4% and +3.6%). Simulated eco-routing benefits can depend more on how the network is coded than on the controller; we argue that such evaluations need matched protocols, placebo controls and network-coding checks. All quantities are simulated tailpipe emissions, not air quality or exposure.
