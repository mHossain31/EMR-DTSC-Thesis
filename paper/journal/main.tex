\begin{abstract}
\noindent Microscopic simulation is widely used to estimate how much rerouting could lower urban traffic emissions,
but such estimates are only as good as the protocol and the network behind them. A 2024 student project reported
that machine-learning-triggered rerouting cut simulated NO$_x$ in Flushing, Queens, by \LegacyNOxDropRF\%. Auditing
its notebook, we find that the comparison set a run of about \LegacyBaselineHorizon~s against runs stopped after
\LegacyControlledSteps~s and that its reroute calls returned unchanged routes; replicated under a matched protocol
on \LegacyTestN\ seeds per demand level, the same rule is equivalent to no rerouting. We then test emission-aware
rerouting driven by a \HorizonS-second traffic-state forecast in SUMO under a preregistered, paired, multi-seed
design with travel-time and placebo baselines. On the network as built from OpenStreetMap it changed total NO$_x$
by \PCTwoLightMean\%, \PCTwoModerateMean\% and \PCTwoHeavyMean\% against no rerouting and by \PCThreeLightMean\%,
\PCThreeModerateMean\% and \PCThreeHeavyMean\% against travel-time rerouting (light, moderate, heavy demand), at a
travel-time cost of a few percent. The reduction comes \CompositionWord\ from moving traffic onto residential and
service streets, whose NO$_x$ changed by \ResidNOxLight\%, \ResidNOxModerate\% and \ResidNOxHeavy\%, and the forecaster
\ForecastVerdictR\ a per-edge historical average of the emission rate it supplies. In a sensitivity experiment
specified before its runs, recoding the network with New York City's default 25~mph limit and stop control at
minor-street junctions reversed the effect of measured-emission rerouting against travel-time rerouting
(\SensEcoReactiveVsTtAdaptiveTotalNoxGLightPosted\%, \SensEcoReactiveVsTtAdaptiveTotalNoxGModeratePosted\% and
\SensEcoReactiveVsTtAdaptiveTotalNoxGHeavyPosted\%). Simulated eco-routing benefits can depend more on how the network
is coded than on the controller; we argue that such evaluations need matched protocols, placebo controls and
network-coding checks. All quantities are simulated tailpipe emissions, not air quality or exposure.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} eco-routing; dynamic rerouting; traffic emissions; NO$_x$; SUMO; preregistration;
reproducibility; network representation

\section{Introduction}
Routing vehicles away from congested or high-emitting streets is an attractive lever for lowering urban traffic
emissions, because it needs no new infrastructure. Its effect is hard to measure on real streets, so it is usually
estimated in microscopic traffic simulation, where every vehicle's route and emissions can be controlled and
recorded. Simulation makes such interventions cheap to test, and also easy to mismeasure: a comparison can differ in
more than the intervention, an intervention can silently do nothing, and a result can rest on modelling choices
that nobody varied.

This paper starts from a case in point. A 2024 student project at the New York Institute of Technology modelled
downtown Flushing, Queens, in SUMO and reported that rerouting triggered by a machine-learning NO$_x$ predictor
reduced simulated NO$_x$ by \LegacyNOxDropRF\% (random forest) and \LegacyNOxDropXGB\% (XGBoost). We audited the
project's notebook and found that the result could not survive two questions: were the compared runs the same
length, and did the intervention change anything? We then rebuilt the study to ask the substantive question under a
protocol that removes those failure modes. Following the preregistration, we address four research questions:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \textbf{RQ1 (measurement validity).} How much of the legacy-style reduction is explained by protocol
artifacts rather than by an effect of the intervention?
\item \textbf{RQ2 (forecasting).} Can the next minute's edge-level NO$_x$ rate be forecast from traffic-state
features better than persistence and historical baselines, on held-out runs?
\item \textbf{RQ3 (control).} Does emission-aware rerouting driven by such forecasts reduce network NO$_x$ compared
with no rerouting, travel-time rerouting, a random-rerouting placebo and measurement-driven eco-routing, and at what
cost in travel time?
\item \textbf{RQ5 (side effects).} Does it displace emissions onto lower-class streets?\footnote{RQ4 of the
preregistration (value of prediction under measurement noise) belongs to the optional second tier and was not run.}
\end{itemize}
After the preregistered analysis, an adversarial review identified the road network's speed coding as a likely
driver of the RQ3 result, and we added a sensitivity experiment, specified and tagged before its runs, that recodes
the network the way New York City regulates its streets.

\paragraph{Contributions.} (i) An audit of a published-style simulation result that identifies an unmatched-horizon
artifact and a no-op intervention, confirmed by replication. (ii) A preregistered, paired, multi-seed evaluation of
forecast-driven eco-rerouting with travel-time, placebo and measurement-driven baselines, in which every reported
number is generated from result files. (iii) A decomposition showing that the simulated reduction is mostly a shift
of traffic onto residential streets. (iv) A sensitivity experiment showing that the sign of the eco-routing effect
depends on network coding, with practical recommendations for simulation-based evaluations.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Eco-routing.} The route a driver takes, or the fastest one, is not always the one that minimises fuel use
or emissions. For real trips in Lund, \citet{ericsson2006fuel} found that drivers often did not take the most
fuel-efficient route and estimated the saving a route-choice support tool could offer; \citet{ahn2008route}
quantified the effect of route choice on energy use and emissions with microscopic models. At the network level,
\citet{ahn2013ecorouting} evaluated eco-routing strategies in a large-scale case study.
\citet{boriboonsomsin2012ecorouting} described an eco-routing navigation system that fuses historical and real-time
traffic data with emission factors for different vehicles and road conditions.

\paragraph{Learning-based control and forecasting.} Machine learning has been applied to traffic control with
attention to emissions, for example reinforcement-learning signal control evaluated on air-quality indicators
\citep{fazzini2022signals,haydari2021signals}, and to dynamic rerouting aimed at travel efficiency
\citep{du2021rerouting}. These studies optimise or predict; the question we ask is whether the prediction adds
information beyond simple baselines once it is embedded in a controller.

\paragraph{Simulation tools, network data and evaluation methodology.} We use SUMO \citep{lopez2018sumo}, which
provides HBEFA-based emission models and PHEMlight \citep{krajzewicz2015emissions}; we use its HBEFA~4.2 classes.
Networks come from OpenStreetMap, which \citet{zilske2011osm} found a valuable source for traffic simulation while
noting that attributes such as speeds and junction layout must be derived or assumed, the kind of gap our
sensitivity experiment probes. Methodologically, we draw on preregistration to separate confirmatory from exploratory
analysis \citep{nosek2018prereg}, on equivalence testing to support claims of no meaningful effect
\citep{schuirmann1987tost,lakens2017equivalence}, on the Wilcoxon signed-rank test \citep{wilcoxon1945}, BCa
bootstrap intervals \citep{efron1987bca} and Holm's procedure \citep{holm1979}. We gradient-boost with LightGBM
\citep{ke2017lightgbm}.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}
\subsection{Matched protocol}
Every condition of a seed shares the network, a byte-identical route file, the fleet, the SUMO seed and the options,
and every run continues until all vehicles have arrived. A run is valid if at least \ValidCompletionPct\% of its
vehicles complete their trips and at most \ValidTeleportPct\% are teleported. The primary outcome is total NO$_x$
from SUMO's per-trip emission output; travel time, its 95th percentile, departure delay, vehicle-kilometres and
CO$_2$ are secondary.

\subsection{Controllers}
Table~\ref{tab:controllers} lists the six conditions. The three eco-routing controllers set a routing weight on every
edge every \ReroutePeriodS~s,
\begin{equation}
w_e = (1-\alpha)\,\tau_e + \alpha\,\bar\tau\,\frac{m_e}{\bar m},
\end{equation}
where $\tau_e$ is the edge's travel time from its time-mean speed over the last \TauAvgS~s (floored at free-flow
time, as SUMO's rerouting device averages), $m_e$ the NO$_x$ a vehicle emits traversing the edge, and bars denote
network means; each vehicle is rerouted every \ReroutePeriodS~s with SUMO's travel-time router on these weights, so
that at $\alpha = 0$ the controllers reduce to travel-time rerouting. $\alpha^*$ was chosen on validation seeds as the
value minimising NO$_x$ with mean travel time at most \AlphaTTBudgetPct\% above travel-time rerouting's.

\subsection{Forecaster}
Gradient-boosted trees forecast each edge's NO$_x$ per vehicle-second, mean speed and NO$_x$ density for the next
minute from sensor-like features only (vehicle counts, speeds, halting, waiting time, their lags and their upstream
and downstream neighbours, and static attributes), with no emission information. They are trained on runs under a
mix of controllers, so that the training data contain controller-induced traffic states, and tuned on validation
seeds. Baselines are persistence, an exponential moving average, the per-edge historical mean of the training data
and ridge regression.

\subsection{Experimental design and preregistration}
Seeds are split into disjoint sets for the pilot, training, validation and test (Table~\ref{tab:design}). The
comparisons, the $\alpha^*$ selection rule, the test seeds, the sample size and the analysis code (by hash) were
frozen in a tagged commit before any test seed ran. The pilot's spread of paired changes (SD \PilotSD\%) gave
$n = \ConfirmatoryN$ seeds per condition and level, the cap of the preregistered rule.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
For each seed we compute the percent change of total NO$_x$ against the reference condition. Four preregistered
comparisons are tested at each demand level: PC1 (legacy rule vs no rerouting) and PC4 (forecast vs measured
eco-routing) with two one-sided tests for equivalence within $\pm$\SESOI\%, PC2 (forecast eco-routing vs no
rerouting) and PC3 (vs travel-time rerouting) with two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. The \TestsInFamily\ tests
form one Holm family at \FamilywiseAlpha. Intervals are BCa bootstrap intervals. At $n = \ConfirmatoryN$ the planned
power was \PowerAtNSup\% for a true change of \SESOI\%; on the test seeds the SDs of paired changes were
\ObservedSDMin\% to \ObservedSDMax\%, so the realised precision was much higher. Everything beyond PC1--PC4 is
exploratory.

\subsection{Legacy replication}
The legacy rule is reproduced as condition ``legacy rule'' (Table~\ref{tab:controllers}), with its random forest
retrained on the same kind of records and its threshold expressed as the training-NO$_x$ quantile at which the 2024
run flagged vehicle-steps. Its ``legacy metric'' sums every NO$_x$ record of a full no-rerouting run against the
rerouting run's first \LegacyControlledSteps~s, as the notebook did; the ``matched metric'' compares complete runs.

\subsection{Network-coding sensitivity experiment}\label{sec:sensdesign}
Because the RQ3 effect turned out to depend on detours through residential streets, we added an experiment,
specified and tagged before its runs, on a recoded copy of the network (Table~\ref{tab:networks}, ``NYC-coded''). The
\SensEdgesSpeedChanged\ edges that carried a road-type default speed get New York City's default limit of 25~mph,
which applies to streets without a posted limit \citep{nycdot2014speed}; the \SensEdgesTagged\ edges with an OSM
speed tag keep it; and the \SensJunctionsStop\ unsignalised junctions entered by a residential, unclassified or
service street get a stop on their minor approaches (a modelling assumption, not the city's sign inventory). Demand
was recalibrated to the same targets (Table~\ref{tab:demand}). No rerouting, travel-time rerouting and
measured-emission eco-routing ($\alpha^* = \AlphaStarReactive$) were run on the \ConfirmatoryN\ test seed numbers;
forecast eco-routing was not, because its forecaster takes the speed limits as inputs. The pre-specified reading:
the reduction ``persists'' at a level if the 95\% interval of eco-routing (measured) vs travel-time rerouting lies
below zero.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
\subsection{RQ1: the legacy result is a measurement artifact}
In the 2024 notebook the rerouting runs covered \LegacyRowRatioRF\% as many vehicle-steps as the baseline, and
\LegacyRepeatUnchanged\ of \LegacyRepeatCalls\ repeated reroute calls returned the unchanged route. Its predictor is
near-deterministic ($R^2 = \LegacyRFRTwo$ on a random split, \LegacyRFRunSplitRTwo\ split by run) because NO$_x$ is
predicted from other outputs of the same emission model at the same instant. Replicated under the matched protocol,
the legacy metric still reports a large drop while the matched metric shows none (Table~\ref{tab:legacy}), and the
rule is close to a no-op: \LegacyRecountChanges\ of \LegacyRecountCalls\ reroute calls changed a vehicle's path
(\LegacyRecountRate\%). The preregistered PC1 confirms equivalence to no rerouting at every level
(Table~\ref{tab:pc}).

\subsection{RQ5: the reduction is a shift onto residential streets}
Per-edge NO$_x$ shows where the reduction comes from (Table~\ref{tab:spatial}, Figure~\ref{fig:roadclass}, top row).

\subsection{RQ2: the forecast adds little over a per-edge average}
On \TestRuns\ test-seed runs the forecaster clearly beats persistence, but for NO$_x$ per vehicle-second, the
quantity the controller's emission cost uses, it \ForecastVerdictR\ the per-edge historical mean
(Table~\ref{tab:forecast}). It beats the historical mean for speed and NO$_x$ density, and its hotspot ranking is
better than the baselines'.

\subsection{Network-coding sensitivity: the effect reverses}
On the NYC-coded network, measured eco-routing no longer lowers NO$_x$ against travel-time rerouting: at every
level its 95\% interval lies above zero (Table~\ref{tab:sens}, Figure~\ref{fig:sens}). Against no rerouting its
reduction shrinks or vanishes, while travel-time rerouting now lowers NO$_x$ more than on the original network. With
the default limit on every urban street, free-flow routes already use residential streets heavily
(Figure~\ref{fig:roadclass}, bottom row), and eco-routing no longer shifts NO$_x$ onto them. Its travel-time cost
grew. Of the 540 runs, \SensInvalidRuns\ failed the validity rule (pairs excluded). By the pre-specified reading, the
reduction does not persist at any level. Light and moderate demand, where both networks are equally congested, show
this as clearly as heavy demand, where the recoded network is less congested.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\paragraph{Principal findings.} The 2024 reduction was produced by its measurement: a full baseline was compared
with truncated runs, and the intervention did not change routes. Under a matched, preregistered protocol, forecast
eco-routing reduced simulated network NO$_x$ on the network as built, at a modest travel-time cost, and beat a
placebo matched in route-change rate. But the reduction is mostly a relocation of traffic onto residential streets,
and on a network coded like New York City's streets the same kind of controller increases NO$_x$ relative to plain
travel-time rerouting.

\paragraph{Why network coding matters.} An eco-router minimises the emission cost of a route. In the OSM-derived
network, arterials carry SUMO's type-default speeds of \SpeedTertiary--\SpeedPrimSec~km/h and stop at signals, so their per-vehicle
emission cost is high, while residential streets are coded at \SpeedResid~km/h with no stop control, which makes them cheap.
The router exploits exactly that contrast. With 25~mph on both arterials and residential streets, and stops on minor
approaches, the contrast largely disappears: residential streets are already used by free-flow routes, and detours
add stop-and-go and distance without a lower emission rate. The effect of the controller is therefore inseparable
from how the network encodes speed and junction control, two attributes that OpenStreetMap often lacks and
conversion tools fill with defaults.

\paragraph{Implications for evaluating eco-routing.} Simulation studies of eco-routing should (i) compare runs of
equal scope, (ii) verify that the intervention changes behaviour, (iii) include travel-time and placebo baselines,
(iv) report where emissions move, not only totals, and (v) test the result against the network attributes it depends
on, such as speed limits and junction control. A routing objective that counts only total network NO$_x$ rewards
displacement onto residential streets; any deployment-oriented study needs an exposure-aware objective and dispersion
modelling, which this study does not have.

\paragraph{The forecast.} The controller's emission cost uses forecast NO$_x$ per vehicle-second, for which the
forecaster does not beat a per-edge historical average. Forecast eco-routing emitted less than measured eco-routing
at some levels, but no preregistered condition isolates the forecast, and a learned per-edge prior is a plausible
source of that difference; we do not attribute it to forecasting.

\section{Threats to validity}\label{sec:threats}
\paragraph{Construct validity.} The outcome is simulated tailpipe emission mass from European (HBEFA) emission
models, not concentration, exposure or health. Road-class results cover edges, not junction lanes.
\paragraph{Internal validity.} Conditions are paired by seed and share route files; the analysis code was frozen
before the test seeds; post-hoc additions are labelled exploratory and listed in the project's deviation log, among
them a corrected route-change counter for the legacy replication. Departure delay is reported apart from travel time.
\paragraph{External validity.} Demand is synthetic and not calibrated to traffic counts; it lasts one hour; drivers
comply fully; the network comes from OpenStreetMap, and its coding changes the result (Section~\ref{sec:sensdesign}).
The stop control of the recoded network is an assumption, forecast eco-routing was not tested on it, and neither
coding was validated against measured speeds. Only one district was studied.
\paragraph{Statistical conclusion validity.} Intervals are narrow because runs are paired and seeds many; the
sensitivity experiment is exploratory and uncorrected for multiplicity, and its heavy-demand comparison mixes coding
and congestion (Table~\ref{tab:demand}).

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
An audit and a matched replication showed that reported simulated NO$_x$ reductions of \LegacyNOxDropRF\% and
\LegacyNOxDropXGB\% from ML-triggered rerouting were a measurement artifact. A preregistered rebuild found that emission-aware rerouting does lower
simulated NO$_x$ on the network as built, but mostly by moving traffic onto residential streets, with a forecaster
that adds little over a per-edge average, and a sensitivity experiment showed that the effect reverses when the
network is coded like New York City's streets. The robust contribution is methodological: simulated eco-routing
benefits should be reported together with matched protocols, placebo controls, the spatial redistribution of
emissions and their sensitivity to network coding.

\section{Deviations from the preregistration}
The deviation log records every departure with its reason and effect. Those bearing on the reported results:
the effort controllers use device-equivalent travel times and SUMO's travel-time router on global weights, adopted
after the $\alpha = 0$ sanity check failed in the pilot (before the freeze); the NO$_x$-rate forecaster is fitted on
its own scale (before the freeze); after the results, an adversarial review led to reporting additions (side effects,
forecast skill on test seeds, tail travel time, departure delay, the legacy replication on test seeds) with the
preregistered tests unchanged, and to a corrected route-change counter for the legacy rule; and the network-coding
sensitivity experiment was added at the author's request, specified and tagged before its runs.

\section{Legacy audit}
The audit of the 2024 notebook (eight hypotheses, each with evidence and status) found: an unmatched horizon (the
baseline file spans about \LegacyBaselineHorizon~s, the rerouting runs stop at \LegacyControlledSteps~steps, and
summing all records alone reproduces the reported drop); no-op rerouting (all repeated calls returned the unchanged
route); a circular prediction task (NO$_x$ predicted from the same vehicle's other pollutants at the same instant,
random row split); a threshold whose units were stated three different ways; claims of averaging over several runs
without evidence; hard-coded stand-in values left in the notebook; and four references that could not be found. No legacy
reference is reused here.
